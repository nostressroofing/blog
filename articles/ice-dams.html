How to Prevent Ice Dams in a Prairie Winter

Saskatoon roofing advice · Winter

Every February in Saskatoon, the same thing happens: big ridges of ice build up along eaves, water starts dripping inside, and roofers' phones start ringing. Ice dams are probably the single most common winter roofing problem in the city, and they're almost always preventable.

Why they form

An ice dam starts in your attic, not on your roof. Heat escaping from your house warms the underside of the roof deck. Snow on the upper part of the roof melts, runs down, and refreezes when it hits the cold overhang past your exterior walls. That ridge of ice grows with every melt-freeze cycle, and eventually the water pooling behind it has nowhere to go but under your shingles and into your house.

Saskatoon's climate is perfect for this: long stretches well below freezing, then a sunny −5°C afternoon that melts the top layer just enough. Older homes in Caswell Hill, Nutana, and City Park are especially prone because their attics were insulated to 1970s standards.

What actually fixes it

The real fix is keeping your attic cold — the same temperature as outside. That comes down to two things:

  • Insulation. Most Saskatoon attics should have R-50 or better. If you can see your ceiling joists, you don't have enough. Blown-in cellulose or fibreglass over the existing insulation is usually a straightforward job.
  • Ventilation. Soffit vents let cold air in low, ridge or box vents let warm air out high. If your soffits are blocked by insulation (very common), the whole system stalls. Baffles at the eaves keep the airflow path open.

Seal air leaks too — the hatch to your attic, pot lights, and plumbing stacks are all little chimneys pumping warm air upward. A tube of caulk and some weatherstripping around the attic hatch costs almost nothing.

Where heat cables fit in

Heating cables along the eaves are a reasonable band-aid, not a cure. They melt channels through the ice so water can drain instead of backing up. They do work — but if a house relies on cables every winter, the underlying insulation or ventilation problem is still there, costing heating dollars all season.

What to do right now

If you've got dams forming this winter: don't hack at them with an axe or a shovel. You'll wreck your shingles. A roof rake can pull snow off the lower few feet of roof safely from the ground, which stops the dam from growing. For ice that's already there, calcium chloride in a nylon stocking laid across the dam will melt a drainage channel — never rock salt, which corrodes flashing and kills the grass below.

Then in spring, get into the attic (or get someone in there) and look at insulation depth and ventilation. Fixing the cause once beats fighting the symptom every February.
